1. Solutions

Solution

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components. The component present in larger amount is generally called the solvent, while the other component is the solute.

Molarity

M = Number of moles of solute / Volume of solution in litres

Molality

m = Number of moles of solute / Mass of solvent in kg

Mole Fraction

Xₐ = nₐ / (nₐ + nᵦ)

Mass Percentage

Mass % = (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100

Raoult's Law

Pₐ = XₐPₐ°
Exam Tip: Molarity temperature ke saath change ho sakti hai, jabki molality generally temperature independent hoti hai.

2. Electrochemistry

Electrochemical Cell

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy or electrical energy into chemical energy.

Cell Potential

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Nernst Equation

E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q

The above form is commonly used at 25°C.

Gibbs Energy and Cell Potential

ΔG° = −nFE°

Conductance

G = 1/R

Faraday's First Law

The mass of a substance deposited during electrolysis is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte.

Q = It
Remember: Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode.

3. Chemical Kinetics

Rate of Reaction

Rate = Change in concentration / Time

Rate Law

Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

Order of Reaction

Order = m + n

First Order Reaction

k = (2.303/t) log(a/(a-x))

Half-Life

t₁/₂ = 0.693/k

For a first-order reaction, half-life does not depend on the initial concentration.

Arrhenius Equation

k = A e⁻ᴱᵃ/ᴿᵀ
Important: Temperature increase generally increases the rate constant because more molecules can cross the activation-energy barrier.

4. d- and f-Block Elements

Transition Elements

Elements having partially filled d-orbitals in their atoms or common oxidation states are generally called transition elements.

Important Properties

  • Variable oxidation states
  • Formation of coloured ions
  • Complex formation
  • Catalytic activity
  • Magnetic behaviour

Lanthanides

Lanthanides involve filling of the 4f subshell.

Actinides

Actinides involve filling of the 5f subshell. Many actinides are radioactive.

Remember: Variable oxidation states are an important characteristic of transition elements.

5. Coordination Compounds

Coordination Compound

A coordination compound contains a central metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands.

Important Terms

  • Central metal atom/ion
  • Ligand
  • Coordination number
  • Coordination sphere
Coordination Number = Number of donor atoms directly attached to central metal

Ligand

A ligand is an ion or molecule capable of donating an electron pair to the central metal ion.

Example:
[Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ has Cu²⁺ as the central metal ion and NH₃ as ligand.

Applications

  • Biological systems
  • Medicines
  • Analytical chemistry
  • Metal extraction

6. Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

General Form

R−X

Here X can be F, Cl, Br or I.

Nucleophilic Substitution

R−X + OH⁻ → R−OH + X⁻

Important Reactions

R−X + KOH(aq) → R−OH + KX
R−X + KOH(alc.) → Alkene + KX + H₂O

Uses

  • Organic synthesis
  • Solvents
  • Industrial chemicals
Exam Tip: Aqueous KOH generally favours substitution, while alcoholic KOH commonly favours elimination.

7. Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Alcohol

R−OH

Phenol

C₆H₅−OH

Ether

R−O−R'

Oxidation of Primary Alcohol

Primary Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic Acid

Reaction of Alcohol with Sodium

2ROH + 2Na → 2RONa + H₂

Williamson Ether Synthesis

RONa + R'X → ROR' + NaX
Important: Phenol is more acidic than ordinary alcohols because the phenoxide ion is resonance stabilised.

8. Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

Aldehyde

R−CHO

Ketone

R−CO−R'

Carboxylic Acid

R−COOH

Oxidation

Aldehyde + [O] → Carboxylic Acid

Reduction

Aldehyde/Ketone + [H] → Alcohol

Esterification

RCOOH + R'OH ⇌ RCOOR' + H₂O
Remember: Aldehydes generally oxidise more easily than ketones.

9. Amines

Amine

Amines are derivatives of ammonia in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups.

Types

  • Primary amine: R−NH₂
  • Secondary amine: R₂NH
  • Tertiary amine: R₃N

Basic Nature

Amines show basic behaviour because nitrogen has a lone pair of electrons that can accept a proton.

Diazotisation

Aniline + NaNO₂ + HCl → Benzene diazonium chloride
Exam Tip: Primary aromatic amines are important in diazonium-salt reactions.

10. Biomolecules

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic compounds containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with many containing hydrogen and oxygen in approximately the ratio 2:1.

  • Glucose
  • Fructose
  • Sucrose
  • Starch
  • Cellulose

Proteins

Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.

Peptide Bond

−CO−NH−

Vitamins

  • Water-soluble: B-group, C
  • Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K

DNA and RNA

  • DNA stores genetic information.
  • RNA participates in protein synthesis and other cellular processes.

11. Polymers

Polymer

A polymer is a large molecule formed by repeated joining of smaller units called monomers.

Addition Polymerisation

n(CH₂=CH₂) → (−CH₂−CH₂−)ₙ

Ethene forms polyethylene.

Examples

  • Polyethylene
  • PVC
  • Teflon
  • Nylon
  • Bakelite
Remember: Monomer is the smaller repeating unit from which a polymer is formed.

12. Chemistry in Everyday Life

Drugs

Chemicals used for diagnosis, prevention or treatment of diseases are commonly discussed as drugs in medicinal chemistry.

Antacids

Antacids help neutralise excess acid in the stomach.

Antiseptics

Antiseptics are substances used on living tissues to reduce or prevent the growth of microorganisms.

Disinfectants

Disinfectants are generally used on non-living surfaces to control microorganisms.

Soaps and Detergents

Soaps are salts of higher fatty acids. Detergents are synthetic cleansing agents.

Important: Soap cleaning action depends on formation of micelles in water.
