MODULE 1 — Quantum Mechanics

1. Introduction to Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the behaviour of matter and radiation at microscopic scales such as atoms, electrons and other particles.

Classical mechanics cannot completely explain many microscopic phenomena. Quantum mechanics provides a mathematical description of such systems.

2. Wave Nature of Particles

According to de Broglie's hypothesis, a moving particle has an associated wave called a matter wave.

de Broglie wavelength:

λ = h / p

For a particle of mass m and velocity v:

λ = h / mv

where h is Planck's constant.

Important idea:

A particle having greater momentum has a smaller de Broglie wavelength.

3. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The uncertainty principle states that it is impossible to determine the exact position and exact momentum of a microscopic particle simultaneously.

Δx Δp ≥ ħ / 2

where
ħ = h / 2π

Since p = mv:

Δx Δv ≥ ħ / 2m

4. Operators in Quantum Mechanics

An operator is a mathematical operation used on a wavefunction to obtain a physical quantity.

Momentum operator:

p̂ = -iħ d/dx

Energy operator:

Ê = iħ ∂/∂t

5. Wave Function

The state of a quantum particle is represented by a wave function usually written as ψ.

The wave function itself is not directly measurable. According to Born's interpretation, the probability density is related to:

Probability density = |ψ|²

For a normalized wavefunction:

∫ |ψ|² dτ = 1

6. Schrödinger Wave Equation

The Schrödinger equation is the fundamental equation used to describe the quantum state of a system.

Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ

where Ĥ represents the Hamiltonian operator.

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Ĥψ = Eψ

For a particle in a potential V:

- ħ²/2m × d²ψ/dx² + Vψ = Eψ

7. Particle in One-Dimensional Box

Consider a particle confined inside a one-dimensional box of length L. Inside the box the potential is taken as zero and outside the box it is extremely large.

Energy of nth state:

Eₙ = n²h² / 8mL²

where n = 1, 2, 3, ...

The energy is quantized, meaning that the particle can possess only certain allowed energy values.

Important point:

For ground state n = 1:

E₁ = h² / 8mL²

8. Free Particle and Wave Packets

A free particle is a particle on which no external force is acting. A wave packet can be formed by superposition of waves with different wavelengths.

Group velocity:

vg = dω/dk

Phase velocity:

vp = ω/k

For matter waves, the relation between group and phase velocity is an important result.

MODULE 2 — Wave Optics

1. Huygens' Principle

According to Huygens' principle, every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. The new wavefront is obtained as the common tangent to these secondary wavelets.

2. Superposition of Waves

When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is equal to the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

y = y₁ + y₂

3. Interference of Light

Interference is the redistribution of intensity produced when two coherent light waves superpose.

Two conditions are important:

  • Constructive interference → bright fringe
  • Destructive interference → dark fringe
Constructive:
Path difference = nλ

Destructive:
Path difference = (2n + 1)λ/2

4. Young's Double Slit Experiment

Young's double slit experiment demonstrates the interference nature of light.

Fringe width:

β = λD/d

where:

  • λ = wavelength
  • D = distance between slit and screen
  • d = distance between the two slits
Important:

Fringe width increases when wavelength or screen distance increases. It decreases when slit separation increases.

5. Newton's Rings

Newton's rings are circular interference fringes produced due to the interference of light reflected from the upper and lower surfaces of a thin air film.

For dark rings:

Dₙ² = 4nλR

where R is the radius of curvature of the lens.

6. Michelson Interferometer

Michelson interferometer is an optical instrument based on the principle of interference.

It can be used for measuring wavelength and small differences in optical path.

2d = nλ

The exact relation depends on the experimental arrangement and definition of the displacement used.

7. Mach-Zehnder Interferometer

Mach-Zehnder interferometer uses beam splitting and recombination to produce interference fringes.

It is useful for studying changes in optical path and refractive index.

8. Fraunhofer Diffraction

Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when the source and observation point are effectively at large distances from the diffracting aperture, or when suitable lenses are used.

Single Slit Diffraction

Condition for minima:

a sinθ = nλ

n = 1, 2, 3, ...

Here a is the width of the slit.

9. Circular Aperture

Diffraction from a circular aperture limits the resolving ability of an optical instrument.

10. Rayleigh Criterion

Rayleigh criterion gives a condition for the minimum angular separation at which two point sources can just be resolved.

θ = 1.22 λ/D

where D is the diameter of the circular aperture.

11. Diffraction Grating

A diffraction grating consists of a large number of equally spaced parallel slits.

Grating equation:

d sinθ = nλ

where d is the grating element.

12. Resolving Power of Grating

Resolving power describes the ability of a grating to distinguish two wavelengths that are close to each other.

Resolving Power:

R = λ/Δλ = nN

where n is the order of spectrum and N is the number of illuminated lines.

MODULE 3 — Optical and Electromagnetic Concepts

1. Polarization of Light

Polarization is the phenomenon in which the vibrations of a transverse light wave are restricted to a particular direction.

Malus' Law:

I = I₀ cos²θ

2. Brewster's Law

When unpolarized light is incident at Brewster's angle, the reflected light becomes plane polarized.

μ = tan iₚ

where iₚ is the polarizing angle.

3. Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves consist of mutually perpendicular electric and magnetic fields, both perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀)

MODULE 4 — Lasers

1. Laser

LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

Laser light is highly directional and can have high coherence and monochromaticity.

2. Absorption and Emission

Atoms can interact with electromagnetic radiation through absorption, spontaneous emission and stimulated emission.

  • Absorption: Atom absorbs energy.
  • Spontaneous emission: Excited atom emits radiation naturally.
  • Stimulated emission: An incoming photon causes emission of another photon with related properties.

3. Population Inversion

Population inversion is the condition in which the number of atoms in the higher energy state becomes greater than the number in the lower energy state for the relevant laser transition.

4. Applications of Lasers

  • Optical communication
  • Industrial measurements
  • Scientific research
  • Medical applications
  • Barcode and scanning systems

MODULE 5 — Solid State Physics

1. Crystal Structure

A crystal is a solid in which atoms, ions or molecules are arranged in a regular repeating pattern.

2. Unit Cell

The unit cell is the smallest repeating geometrical unit that can represent the crystal structure.

3. Bragg's Law

Bragg's law gives the condition for constructive interference of X-rays reflected from crystal planes.

2d sinθ = nλ

where:

  • d = spacing between crystal planes
  • θ = glancing angle
  • λ = wavelength
  • n = order of diffraction

4. Semiconductor

A semiconductor has electrical conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator.

Examples include silicon and germanium.

5. Intrinsic Semiconductor

A pure semiconductor without intentional impurity addition is called an intrinsic semiconductor.

6. Extrinsic Semiconductor

A semiconductor whose conductivity is modified by adding suitable impurities is called an extrinsic semiconductor.

  • N-type semiconductor
  • P-type semiconductor
